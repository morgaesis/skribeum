# Literature review: shared workspace design

Cedar Room's pilot draws on published guidance on shared community
workspaces, gathered before the layout is fixed. Each section closes
with what it means for [[Examples/Work/decision-log|the decision log]].

## Why a literature review before a pilot

A pilot answers "does this work for us." A literature review answers
"what has already been learned by people who ran a dozen pilots before us."
Skipping it means re-discovering the same failure modes at Cedar Room's
expense instead of a stranger's.

## Method

The sources behind this review are general-purpose workspace and
facilities guidance, not a single controlled study. None describe Cedar
Room specifically. Each recommendation below is included only where it
recurred across multiple independent sources rather than appearing once
and getting repeated by everything that cited it. Where sources
disagreed outright, that disagreement is noted instead of averaged away,
because an averaged recommendation is often a recommendation nobody
actually tested.

## Acoustic zoning

Open community rooms fail most often on noise, not seating. Three
recurring recommendations appear across the sources:

- Separate "quiet work" and "conversation" zones by at least one full room
  width, not just a partition.
- Soft materials (rugs, upholstered seating, acoustic panels) reduce
  perceived noise more than physical distance alone.
- A booked, enclosed room for calls beats an open "phone booth" nook in
  every account that measured actual usage.

> [!tip] Cedar Room application
> The [[Examples/Research/interview-notes|interview notes]] already flag
> this as the top concern. Acoustic zoning should be a constraint in the
> layout, not a nice-to-have.

## Daylight and orientation

Daylight access correlates with self-reported satisfaction more strongly
than square footage per person in every study reviewed. The practical
guidance converges on:

- Seating within four meters of a window for at least half of all desks.
- Avoiding screens directly facing unshaded south-facing glass.
- Supplementing, not replacing, daylight with tunable warm and cool
  electric light for late-afternoon use.

## Furniture flexibility

| Approach | Setup time | Failure mode observed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed desks, assigned | None | Underused outside business hours |
| Modular tables, casters | 5-10 min | Configuration drifts to one fixed layout within weeks without a reset ritual |
| Folding or nesting furniture | 15-20 min | Abandoned if storage is inconvenient |

The middle option matches Cedar Room's constraints best, provided a
weekly reset is scheduled rather than assumed. Sources that tracked
outcomes over a full year, rather than just the opening weeks, were more
skeptical of full flexibility than the early-adoption literature
suggests: novelty wears off, and a layout that requires effort to
reconfigure tends toward whichever configuration was used most recently
unless a specific person owns the reset.

## Governance and booking

Shared spaces succeed or fail on governance more than design. Recurring
findings:

- A single visible calendar beats a request-based booking form for
  spaces used more than twice a week.
- Unenforced quiet hours are worse than no stated quiet hours, because
  they create a false expectation.
- Volunteer-run spaces need a named point of contact, not a rotating
  roster, for at least the first six months.

Governance sources also agreed, with unusual consistency, that written
rules matter less than who is present to model them. A posted quiet-hours
sign with nobody willing to have an awkward conversation performs worse
than an unwritten norm that a regular visitor reliably reinforces. This
argues for recruiting and briefing a small group of repeat visitors
before the pilot opens, rather than relying on signage alone.

## Accessibility baseline

Every source treated accessibility as a baseline requirement, not a
later addition. The minimum bar across sources: step-free entry, a route
width that accommodates two wheelchairs passing, and at least one
height-adjustable work surface per zone. Spaces described as
`accessible-by-default` scored far higher on long-term satisfaction than
spaces described as `accessible-on-request` in every comparison found.

## What this changes about the plan

Cross-referencing this review against
[[Examples/Work/meeting-notes|meeting notes]] suggests two adjustments
worth raising before the next working session: narrowing the open floor
plan to make room for a proper enclosed call room, and scheduling a
recurring Friday furniture reset rather than hoping the modular layout
holds on its own.

## Open questions for the next interview round

- Does the target group prefer a single large quiet zone or several
  small ones?
- Is a phone booth still worth building if a bookable room exists?
- How many accessible height-adjustable desks does the pilot actually
  need on day one versus at capacity?

## Sources worth revisiting

This section intentionally stays short: a literature review is a working
tool, not an archive. Full citations live outside this vault; this note
keeps only what changed a decision. Two categories of source were
excluded on purpose: vendor case studies with no reported failure mode
(a study that never mentions what went wrong is marketing, not
evidence), and single-site anecdotes with no comparison condition. A
future revision of this review should revisit that exclusion once the
Cedar Room pilot itself produces a full year of data, since a site that
matches the pilot's scale and governance model is worth more than ten
general-purpose sources.
